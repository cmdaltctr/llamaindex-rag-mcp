# Photosynthesis in vascular plants

Photosynthesis is the metabolic pathway in which the chloroplast converts
carbon dioxide and water into glucose and molecular oxygen, driven by photons
absorbed by chlorophyll pigments embedded in the thylakoid membrane.

The pathway has two phases. The light-dependent reactions occur in the
thylakoid membrane and produce ATP and NADPH using the photons absorbed by
photosystems II and I. Water is split at photosystem II, releasing O₂ as a
by-product. The light-independent reactions, also called the Calvin cycle,
occur in the stroma and use the ATP and NADPH from the first phase to fix
CO₂ into a three-carbon intermediate (3-PGA) that is reduced to G3P, the
precursor of glucose, sucrose, starch, and the rest of carbohydrate
metabolism.

Stomata in the leaf surface regulate gas exchange. They open in the morning
to admit carbon dioxide and close at midday under heat or drought stress to
limit water loss through transpiration. The trade-off between CO₂ uptake
and water conservation has shaped the evolution of three photosynthetic
pathways — C3, C4, and CAM — each adapted to a different climatic regime.

Productivity at the canopy scale is bounded by the rate at which Rubisco can
fix CO₂. Rubisco is the most abundant protein in the biosphere and also
one of the slowest enzymes; it carries the bulk of the global terrestrial
carbon flux despite a turnover number of only a few molecules per second.

The pathway is the foundation of the food chain and the principal mechanism
by which the atmosphere has been oxygenated. Without it the planet's
atmosphere would still resemble its Hadean composition.
