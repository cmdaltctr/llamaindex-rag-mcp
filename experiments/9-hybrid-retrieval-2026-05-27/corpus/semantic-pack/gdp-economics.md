# Gross domestic product

GDP — gross domestic product — captures the value added by all final goods
and services within an economy in a given accounting period. National
statistical agencies compile GDP from primary surveys and administrative
records and publish quarterly estimates that are revised several times as
more complete data arrives.

There are three equivalent compilation approaches. The expenditure approach
sums consumption (C), investment (I), government spending (G), and net
exports (X − M). The income approach sums wages, profits, rents, and
indirect taxes net of subsidies. The production approach sums gross value
added across industries, with intermediate consumption netted out to avoid
double counting. Reconciling the three approaches is one of the persistent
challenges in national-accounts compilation; the residual is published as a
statistical discrepancy.

Real GDP deflates nominal GDP by a price index — typically the GDP deflator
— to remove the effect of inflation. Real GDP is the headline figure in
macroeconomic policy debates because it tracks volume rather than price.
Per-capita GDP divides through by the population and is the dominant
cross-economy welfare proxy, though it is widely acknowledged to miss
distribution, non-market activity, and environmental degradation.

Compilation standards are harmonised internationally through the System of
National Accounts, currently SNA 2008, with the SNA 2025 revision now in
phased adoption. Eurostat publishes the European-system equivalent (ESA
2010). The IMF, OECD, and World Bank rely on these standards to compile
cross-economy aggregates.

The conceptual gaps in GDP are well documented. It is a flow, not a stock;
it ignores depreciation of natural capital; it values household work at
zero. Alternative aggregates — Genuine Progress Indicator, Human
Development Index, Inclusive Wealth — try to address one or another of
these gaps but none has displaced GDP as the headline aggregate in policy
discourse.
