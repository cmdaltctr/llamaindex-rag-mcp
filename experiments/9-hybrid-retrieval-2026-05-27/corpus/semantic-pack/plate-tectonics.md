# Plate tectonics — a short overview

Plate tectonics is the unifying framework of modern Earth science. The
lithosphere — the rigid outer shell of the planet — is broken into a mosaic
of around fifteen major plates and a few dozen smaller ones, all of which
drift across the underlying asthenosphere at rates of a few centimetres per
year, driven by mantle convection and slab-pull at subducting margins.

Three styles of plate boundary dominate the surface. Divergent boundaries —
the mid-ocean ridges — are where new oceanic lithosphere is created by
upwelling mantle material. Convergent boundaries are where lithosphere is
consumed: oceanic-continental subduction has built the Andes; oceanic-oceanic
subduction has built the island arcs of the western Pacific; and
continental-continental collision has built the Himalaya through the slow
impact of the Indian plate against Eurasia. Transform boundaries — the San
Andreas is the classic example — are where two plates slide past each other
laterally without creation or destruction of crust.

Seismic activity, volcanism, and orogeny all concentrate at plate
boundaries because that is where the lithosphere is under the greatest
mechanical stress. The Wadati-Benioff zone — the inclined band of seismic
activity that traces the descending slab in subduction zones — was one of
the earliest empirical confirmations of the framework in the 1950s.

The framework was assembled in the 1960s from three threads of evidence:
Wegener's much earlier observations of the geometric fit of South America
against Africa; the discovery of magnetic-stripe symmetry across the
mid-ocean ridges; and the systematic mapping of seismic foci. By the late
1960s these threads had cohered into the modern theory, replacing the older
geosynclinal account that had dominated the discipline for half a century.

The framework is now applied at scales from the global to the very local.
It explains the distribution of ore deposits at convergent margins, the
thermal history of sedimentary basins, the rifting that produced the East
African valleys, and the long-term evolution of climate through the
rearrangement of ocean gateways.
