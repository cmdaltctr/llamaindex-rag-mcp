# A short technical overview of EVs

An EV — short for electric vehicle — replaces the petrol or diesel powertrain
with a battery pack, a power electronics module, and one or more electric
motors. The dominant battery chemistry is lithium-ion, with cathode formulas
ranging from NMC (nickel-manganese-cobalt) to LFP (lithium iron phosphate),
each trading energy density against cost and thermal stability.

Energy is measured in kilowatt-hours; modern passenger EVs ship pack
capacities in the 40–110 kWh range. The motor is typically a permanent-magnet
synchronous machine or an induction machine, with peak torque available from
zero rpm — one of the defining drive characteristics of an EV. Regenerative
braking recovers a fraction of the kinetic energy back into the pack during
deceleration, which is why one-pedal driving is feasible in most modern
designs.

Charging happens at three power tiers. Level 1 uses a household outlet and
adds about 5–8 km of range per hour. Level 2 uses a wall-mounted unit at
7–22 kW and is the typical home or office solution. DC fast charging at
50–350 kW bypasses the on-board charger and feeds the pack directly,
restoring most of the pack's usable range in 20–40 minutes for a
well-engineered vehicle.

Range anxiety has been the most-studied behavioural barrier to adoption.
Empirical data from telemetry shows that the average daily commute is well
within the range of even modest packs, but consumer perception lags reality.
Improvements in pack chemistry, fast-charging infrastructure, and route
planning software have closed the gap year on year.

Total cost of ownership now favours the EV across most European and East
Asian markets thanks to lower fuel and maintenance costs, even before
accounting for purchase incentives. The remaining cost gap at the sticker
price has shrunk from roughly 40 % in 2018 to under 10 % in 2025 for the
median family-segment vehicle.
